Most small teams don't plan their infrastructure. They collect it.

A hosting account here, a plugin there, a free tier someone recommended, a tool that was trending the month a project started. Each choice makes sense on its own. Together they become a stack nobody chose on purpose, and the team spends more time keeping it running than building the work it was meant to support.

I know because I did it. I'm Nkululeko Nkosi, known as Leko, and I run Okel Dijital in Durban. This article is an honest account of how I run the technology infrastructure behind our work today, what went wrong when I started, and the lessons I would pass on to any small team. It isn't a "best tools" list. The tools I use fit the work Okel Dijital does now, mostly app development, and they may not fit yours.

## The Short Version

- **Start with the objective, not the tool.** Write down what you are trying to achieve before you look at what is popular.
- **Audit what you already have:** your people, the software you already pay for, and the infrastructure you already run. It often covers more of the goal than you expect.
- **Keep the stack small.** Every extra platform is another login, bill, set of settings and point of failure.
- **Match the tool to the workload.** An app and a content website are different jobs and can need different infrastructure.
- **Scale when you must, not before.** Start on a free tier to learn and prototype, and pay when the product earns or serves customers and real traffic and need justify it.

## My Stack Today, and Why

Much of Okel Dijital's work right now is app development, so the infrastructure I run is built around apps rather than content websites. It has four layers. Here is what each one does, in plain words.

The rule behind every layer is the same: start on the free tier to learn and prototype, and pay when the product earns or serves customers and traffic and need justify it. That applies to hosting, databases and storage alike. When I compare options, I look for three things:

- **Generous pricing**, so a small project isn't penalised for being small.
- **A free tier**, so I can explore and build prototypes before paying for anything.
- **An easy, user-friendly environment**, so setting up and managing it doesn't eat the time I should spend building.

### 1. Hosting: where the app runs (Vercel)

Hosting is the computer, or network of computers, that serves your app to visitors. I host apps on Vercel. Vercel runs a global network that caches static files close to visitors and runs server code ("functions") in a chosen region. Its [region list](https://vercel.com/docs/regions) includes Cape Town (`cpt1`) among 19 compute regions.

I use Vercel's free Hobby plan for discovery and for building prototypes, in line with the start-free rule above. The moment I'm ready to ship a commercial product, I move to a paid plan, because nothing can honestly be shipped commercially on Hobby: Vercel's [Hobby plan page](https://vercel.com/docs/plans/hobby) (accessed 11 October 2026) says the plan "restricts users to non-commercial, personal use only". If what you are building is commercial, check Vercel's current terms and plans before you launch, and plan on a paid plan.

One more detail worth knowing: new projects run functions in Washington, D.C. (`iad1`) by default. Vercel recommends running functions [in the same region as your database](https://vercel.com/docs/functions/configuring-functions/region), or as close as possible. Set the region on purpose rather than accepting the default.

### 2. Database: where the app's records live (Neon or Supabase)

The database stores structured information: user accounts, orders, bookings, settings. I use two managed Postgres services, Neon and Supabase. "Managed" means the provider runs the database server, so I don't have to.

- **Neon**, from Databricks, offers Lakebase Postgres, its managed Postgres, with compute that [scales to zero](https://neon.com/pricing) when idle. Its Free plan is permanent (not a trial); paid plans are billed on usage.
- **Supabase** gives each project a dedicated Postgres database and bundles optional extras such as authentication, file storage, realtime features and edge functions. You can also use it as a [standalone database](https://supabase.com/pricing).

I never use both in the same project. Which one I pick depends on the app I'm building and what it needs, so each project gets one database service, chosen for that project. I chose these two because their pricing is generous, in a similar way to Vercel's, both have free tiers, and both are easy and user-friendly to work in. Those were the plans and prices when I wrote this in October 2026, so check Neon's and Supabase's current pricing pages before you decide.

### 3. Object storage: where users' files live (Cloudflare R2)

Apps also handle files. These are stored separately from the database, in "object storage". I use Cloudflare R2 to store what end users upload to the platforms and SaaS products I build: images, documents and any other media or assets they need to keep. R2 supports the widely used [S3 API](https://developers.cloudflare.com/r2/api/s3/api/), so many existing tools and libraries can talk to it, and Cloudflare states there are [no charges for egress bandwidth](https://developers.cloudflare.com/r2/pricing/), the data sent out to the internet. You pay for storage and for operations (reads and writes) above a monthly free allowance.

### 4. Transactional email: messages the app sends (Resend)

Transactional email is the mail an app sends automatically: sign-up confirmations, password resets, receipts. It is different from your team's own mailboxes. I use Resend for transactional email. It is an email API that also offers SMTP relay and supports [SPF, DKIM and DMARC](https://resend.com/pricing) domain authentication on every plan.

If those three acronyms are new, our guide to [choosing professional email for a small business](/blog/how-to-choose-professional-email-small-business-2026) explains them in plain English. They matter just as much for app mail.

### A South African note: where the parts live

Okel Dijital works from Durban, so geography matters. On 11 October 2026, Vercel listed a Cape Town compute region, but neither Neon nor Supabase listed an African database region, Cloudflare's R2 location hints didn't include Africa, and Resend's sending regions were the US, Ireland, São Paulo and Tokyo.

In practice, an app serving South African users can run its hosting close to them while its database sits on another continent. Because Vercel advises running functions next to the database, the two choices need to be made together. A function in Cape Town that talks to a database in Frankfurt may be slower than one placed beside the database. Measure it for your own app rather than assuming. If you handle personal information, check where each provider stores data too: Supabase notes that choosing a region is "a data-location control, not proof of regulatory compliance".

## What Went Wrong When I Started

My first pilot was my own online store, built to sell web hosting and domains. It looked nothing like the stack above. That store was the pilot for what became the Okel Dijital Store, which is now launching and runs on Next.js rather than WordPress.

I spent a lot of time building the store on WordPress and trying one third-party platform after another. Hosting ran on a reseller hosting package. To be clear, the reseller package itself was fine, and I would use one again for the right job.

The problem was everything around it. My tools were scattered across too many platforms, so building the store took far longer than it should have, and much of that time went into connecting and managing tools rather than the store itself. By the end I had concluded that WordPress, together with the hosting setup I was using then, was not reliable or sustainable for me, especially if I needed to scale.

Looking back, the root cause wasn't WordPress or the host. I chose tools before I had defined what the project needed. Every new platform felt like progress, but each one added setup, another account to manage and another thing that could break. That's tool sprawl, and it is easy to slip into when you are small, busy and keen to move fast.

## The Lessons I Would Pass On

### 1. Define the objective before you look at tools

Write one or two sentences on what you are trying to achieve: "let clients book and pay online", "publish weekly articles that rank". Then list the requirements that follow from it: who will use it, what data it holds, how many users, and who will maintain it. Popular and trending tools are only worth evaluating once those requirements exist.

This is the same principle we apply to AI at Hub Central. Our [framework for evaluating AI tools](/blog/framework-for-evaluating-ai-tools-before-adoption-2026) asks which specific step a tool improves before anything else. Infrastructure deserves the same discipline.

### 2. Audit what you already own

Before buying anything new, take stock of three things:

- **People:** who on the team can build, maintain and troubleshoot what you choose. A tool nobody can support is a liability.
- **Software you already pay for:** existing subscriptions often include features nobody has switched on.
- **Infrastructure you already run:** domains, hosting accounts, DNS, email and storage you could reuse.

Then ask how much of the objective this already covers. Often it covers more than you'd think, and the gap left to fill is smaller than the shopping list you started with.

### 3. Keep the stack small

Each new platform adds an account to secure, a bill to track, settings to document and a place for things to break. That is the cost of [deferring decisions](/blog/the-hidden-cost-of-well-figure-it-out-later-2026): it builds up quietly until something fails. For each layer, aim for one clear tool with one clear job, and write down why you chose it. It's why I pick one database service per project rather than running two side by side.

### 4. Match the tool to the workload

An app and a content website are different jobs. An app needs a place to run code, a database for records, file storage and automated email, which is why my stack looks the way it does. A content site needs good publishing tools, fast pages and an editor non-developers can use. A stack that's right for one can be wrong for the other.

### 5. Scale when you must, not before

My main advice to small teams is simple: don't invest in infrastructure before you have to. Buy capacity when you need more resources, not in anticipation of growth that hasn't arrived. Start on a free or hobby tier to learn and prototype, and pay when the product earns or serves customers, or when your traffic and your needs justify it. That goes for hosting, databases and storage alike.

Vercel is where I draw that line most clearly. I use the Hobby plan to discover and prototype, and the moment I'm ready to ship a commercial product I move to a paid plan, because Hobby is for non-commercial, personal use only. Free is for learning. A product that serves customers should pay its way.

Free tiers have limits, though, and it pays to know what happens when you reach them. As listed on each vendor's page on 11 October 2026 (Neon and Supabase re-checked on 27 October 2026; they may have changed since, so check the current pages):

- **Vercel Hobby:** if you exceed a usage limit, in most cases you have to wait until 30 days have passed before you can use that feature again, and the plan is for non-commercial, personal use only, so it suits prototypes and discovery rather than a shipped commercial product ([Vercel Hobby plan](https://vercel.com/docs/plans/hobby)).
- **Neon Free:** running out of compute hours (100 CU-hours per project) or the 5 GB egress allowance suspends compute until the next billing period, and going over 1 GB of Postgres storage in a project, or 20 GB across all your projects, blocks writes. Neon says none of these limits delete your data ([Neon pricing](https://neon.com/pricing)).
- **Supabase Free:** projects pause after one week of inactivity, and you can have two active projects ([Supabase pricing](https://supabase.com/pricing)).
- **Cloudflare R2:** the free allowance applies only to Standard storage, not Infrequent Access ([R2 pricing](https://developers.cloudflare.com/r2/pricing/)).
- **Resend Free:** 3,000 emails a month, capped at 100 a day ([Resend pricing](https://resend.com/pricing)).

These terms change, so check each vendor's current page before you rely on them, and keep an eye on usage so a limit doesn't surprise you in production. "Scale when you must" only works if you can see the moment you must.

## A Decision Checklist for Small Teams

Use this before adding or replacing any piece of infrastructure.

1. **Objective:** can we state in one sentence what this is for?
2. **Workload:** is this an app, a content site, an online shop or internal tooling?
3. **Existing assets:** have we checked our current people, software and infrastructure first?
4. **Ownership:** who will set it up, maintain it and fix it at short notice?
5. **Fit:** does it do one clear job in our stack, or overlap with something we already have?
6. **Terms:** does the plan allow commercial use, and what happens when we hit a free-tier limit (pause, suspend or bill)?
7. **Location:** where will it run and store data, relative to our users and our other services?
8. **Cost and timing:** do our current traffic and needs justify paying now, and what will it cost at renewal and as usage grows?
9. **Exit:** if we outgrow it, can we export our data and move?

## When WordPress and Traditional Hosting Still Make Sense

My hosting-store pilot didn't work out on WordPress, but that doesn't make WordPress or shared and reseller hosting the wrong choice. For many small teams they are exactly right, and much of Hub Central's own advice is written for them.

They tend to fit when:

- **The site is mainly content:** pages, articles and a contact form, maintained by people who want an editor rather than code.
- **The team already has WordPress skills.** By lesson two, that is an asset to use, not discard.
- **The plan is chosen properly.** Our guide to [choosing the right WordPress hosting plan](/blog/how-to-choose-wordpress-hosting-plan-2026) explains why the renewal price, backups, staging and upgrade path matter more than the launch price.
- **The site is kept lean.** Fewer plugins and a sensible fix order go a long way; see our [WordPress speed checklist](/blog/wordpress-speed-optimization-2026-small-teams).

If you are comparing hosts, start with [best WordPress hosting for small teams](/blog/best-wordpress-hosting-small-teams-2026). What I learned isn't "avoid WordPress". It's "don't let a pile of tools decide your architecture for you".

## What to Do Next

Pick one project and run the checklist above against your current setup. List every platform it touches, write down the job each one does, and look for overlaps, tools nobody owns, and paid plans you don't yet need.

If the project is a WordPress site, our guides to [choosing a hosting plan](/blog/how-to-choose-wordpress-hosting-plan-2026) and [speeding up WordPress](/blog/wordpress-speed-optimization-2026-small-teams) are the next step. If it sends email, start with [how to choose professional email](/blog/how-to-choose-professional-email-small-business-2026).

I'll keep sharing what I learn as my stack changes. The tools will change. The order of the questions shouldn't.
